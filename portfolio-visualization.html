Visualization Pipeline

STAGES 02–04 — LOOK DEV TO VR

Every scene here starts as engineering-accurate CAD, not a free-form model — the job is turning precise, correctly-scaled geometry into something that reads as real and responds correctly once someone is standing next to it in a headset.

Stage 01 SolidWorks Source geometry: instrument housing, mechanism, and control panel, modeled with movable parts kept separate.
Stage 02 Maya Retopology and UV layout tuned for texel density and readability up close in VR, where flaws are far more visible than on a screen.
Stage 03 PBR Texturing Full BaseColor / Metallic / Normal / Roughness sets per part, built to sell material identity — metal, plastic, worn edges — under real-time lighting.
Stage 04 Unreal / VR Scene lighting, interaction wiring for movable parts, and optimization for stable framerate inside a VR headset.

Compact Ripple Tank

Look development & lighting

Problem. CAD-accurate geometry reads as flat and "unfinished" in a real-time viewport — the challenge was making an engineered instrument feel physically real and interactive on screen, not like a mechanical drawing.

Approach. Retopologized and unwrapped clean UVs in Maya per part, then built a full PBR material set (BaseColor, Metallic, Normal, Roughness) so surfaces read correctly under changing light — brushed metal on the housing, painted plastic on the control panel, and worn edges on handled areas. Lit the scene in Unreal with a key/fill/rim setup to separate the moving lid from the static body.

Result. A functional lit scene where the lid and panel switches animate independently, imported from a pipeline-friendly FBX hierarchy, with materials that hold up in a turntable and in motion.

Basic System & Photogates System

Scene assembly & lighting

Problem. A physics track with nine interacting parts — pulley, photogate, fence, rod, cart, cart mass, elastic bumper, track end stop, and the track itself — needed to exist in Unreal not as a static prop, but as a set of independently placeable pieces a trainee could reposition exactly like at a real lab bench.

Approach. Brought each part into Maya keeping the CAD's part separation intact, then staged the full kit in Unreal — track as the base, with the cart, pulley assembly, and both photogates placed and lit as distinct actors rather than baked into one mesh. Lighting was kept clean and neutral, closer to a lab environment than a dramatic product shot, since the priority here is legibility of each component in VR.

Result. A scene where every functional part of the experiment — the piece a trainee needs to grab, slide, or swap — exists as its own object in the engine, ready to be wired for interaction.
